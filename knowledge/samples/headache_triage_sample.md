# Headache Evaluation and Red Flags

## Distinguishing Primary and Secondary Headaches
Headaches are among the most frequent symptoms reported in outpatient and primary care settings. Clinically, they are categorized into primary headaches—such as tension-type headaches, cluster episodes, and migraines—where the headache itself is the underlying medical condition, and secondary headaches, where pain arises as a symptom of an underlying structural, vascular, or infectious process. Tension headaches typically present as a dull, constant, bilateral band of pressure wrapping around the forehead, temples, or back of the neck, commonly provoked by prolonged computer screen exposure, mental stress, sleep deprivation, or neck muscular strain.

## Supportive Self-Care for Tension Headaches
When red flags and focal neurological signs have been reliably excluded, home management for mild tension headaches involves non-pharmacological soothing measures:
- Sensory rest: Resting in a quiet, darkened room away from harsh fluorescent lighting, smartphones, and computer monitors helps relieve sensory overload and ocular fatigue.
- Rehydration therapy: Dehydration is a frequent, under-recognized headache trigger. Slowly drinking 500 mL of clean ambient-temperature water can alleviate discomfort within thirty to sixty minutes.
- Musculoskeletal relief: Applying a warm compress across the base of the skull or neck, or gently massaging the trapezius muscles, can ease localized muscle contraction and pericranial tenderness.
- Screen breaks: Adopting the 20-20-20 rule during prolonged visual desk work (looking at an object 20 feet away for 20 seconds every 20 minutes) reduces ocular strain.

## When to Schedule a Routine or Urgent Doctor Review
A scheduled medical appointment with a general practitioner is recommended when headaches exhibit progressive or changing features:
- Headaches that occur with increasing frequency (more than two to three days per week over several weeks).
- Pain that consistently wakes a person from sound sleep in the early morning hours.
- Headaches accompanied by progressive nausea, morning vomiting, or unexplained weight loss.
- Headaches that begin newly in individuals aged fifty years or older without a prior history of headache disorders.

## Critical Red Flags: Emergency Escalation (SNOOP Criteria)
Emergency medical services (call 112) or immediate hospital attendance must be triggered if a headache displays any of the following high-risk red flags:
- "Thunderclap" onset: Severe pain that reaches peak intensity within 60 seconds (suggesting possible subarachnoid hemorrhage).
- Focal neurological deficits: New onset one-sided arm or leg weakness, facial asymmetry, slurred speech, vision loss, or double vision.
- Systemic infection markers: High fever, neck rigidity, and confusion (suggesting meningitis or encephalitis).
- Post-traumatic headache: Severe, worsening headache developing after a recent blow or injury to the head.
